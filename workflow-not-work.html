The Workflow Is Not the Work

15 July 2026
11 min read

Agentic AI
Cognitive task analysis
Human expertise

What cognitive task analysis reveals about translating human expertise into effective AI agents

Ask an expert how they make a decision and they will usually give you a sensible answer.

They will describe, often with articulate clarity, the information they consider, the steps they follow and the rules that matter in making their decision. For those of us trying to take that information and insert it into an agentic AI system, this may seem fine. We might be considering how to turn this knowledge into instructions for the agent, or deciding if it’s better stored as a collection of documents for retrieval.

There is just one problem, though. And that is, their account is often incomplete.

It’s not because the expert is withholding information from us on purpose. Actually, having expertise changes the explanation a person is able to give. An experienced nurse, for example, may say they “check the patient” without mentioning that they are also registering subtle changes in skin tone, breathing rhythm and responsiveness — cues they have learned to combine through experience without consciously stepping through them. With experience, conscious steps become automatic. Weak signals form recognisable patterns. An unusual detail feels wrong before the expert can say exactly why. The expert learns which rules are reliable, which are provisional and when the apparent task is not the real task at all.

That is why a general question like, “Can you talk me through your process?”, tends to produce the outline of the work rather than the judgment that makes it expert.

Agentic AI has made this psychological phenomenon an engineering problem. We are no longer building systems that only retrieve information or generate text. We are asking them to interpret situations, choose tools, make decisions and act inside real workflows. The agents we are building today have real world stakes with tangible costs associated with getting it wrong. To do this reliably, an agent needs more than access to what an organisation has written down. It needs some representation of how competent people notice, judge and adapt to the task at hand.

This is not a new problem.

Long before large language models, researchers in cognitive science, human factors and knowledge engineering were trying to understand how experts perform complex work. One of the methods they developed was cognitive task analysis. Cognitive task analysis offers a way to recover some of what ordinary process descriptions leave out.

Its central lesson is simple: the workflow is not the cognitive task.

We have encountered this problem before

Cognitive task analysis, or CTA, is a family of methods for identifying the knowledge, judgments and cognitive strategies required to perform complex work. It has been around since the 1980s, and emerged in response to the demands of the workplace at the time.

Traditional task analysis might describe what someone does: inspect a record, compare two values, select an option and escalate the case. CTA asks what makes those actions intelligent.

What did the person notice in the record? What did they expect to find? What made the values meaningfully different rather than merely unequal? Which options did they reject without discussing them? What made this case feel routine? What could make it feel dangerous?

CTA is usually described in three stages:

  1. Knowledge elicitation: interviewing or observing experts as they perform or reconstruct work.
  2. Analysis: identifying cues, goals, decisions, mental models and strategies.
  3. Knowledge representation: turning those findings into a usable representation or model, such as a decision map, requirements table or training aid.

The methods were developed for domains where the formal procedure could not fully explain competent performance: emergency response, aviation, healthcare, engineering and military command. These were environments with uncertainty, time pressure, incomplete information and significant consequences.

They sound increasingly similar to the environments in which we want agents to operate.

The resemblance matters because it changes how we frame the problem. Prompt writing is only one part of turning expertise into an agent. More documents and a newer model will not recover judgments that were never made explicit. This is fundamentally a knowledge-acquisition problem, albeit one with a new consequence: the resulting model can now make and act on decisions.

What the workflow leaves out

Most agent projects begin by mapping a workflow. This is necessary, but also insufficient.

A workflow describes the visible sequence of activity. It tells us what normally happens, which systems are involved and where responsibility moves from one person to another. It rarely explains the cognition that keeps the workflow on course.

Consider an experienced claims assessor reviewing a case. The documented process might say that they verify the evidence, apply the relevant policy and determine an outcome. But their expertise may lie elsewhere:

  • noticing that two individually plausible documents are inconsistent;
  • recognising a pattern associated with a rare exception;
  • knowing which missing information is material;
  • distinguishing an unusual case from a suspicious one;
  • anticipating how a decision will interact with another policy;
  • recognising when the available evidence does not support a confident conclusion.

A process map can include “review the evidence.” It cannot, by itself, tell us what evidence deserves attention or what competent review looks like.

A 2023 study of an LLM-based mathematics tutor shows why this distinction matters. Experienced teachers did more than just respond to an incorrect answer when providing tutoring. They inferred the kind of mistake the student had made, chose an appropriate remediation strategy and decided what the intervention would best help the student understand.

When those intermediate expert decisions were supplied to an LLM, the quality of its responses improved substantially. Without them, the model behaved more like a novice tutor: it gave away the answer or vaguely encouraged the student to try again.

The study shows that the teacher’s final response was only part of what mattered. The useful knowledge lay in how they moved from the student’s answer to a diagnosis and then to a teaching strategy. That reasoning is often missing from agent designs.

Why experts leave things out

Ordinary interviews are particularly weak at revealing this layer.

As people become skilled, parts of their performance becomes automatic. An expert no longer needs to consciously rehearse every distinction or procedural step as it becomes second nature to them. When asked to explain their work, they may omit information a novice would need — not because it is unimportant, but because it has become obvious to them.

Research on surgical instruction, for example, has found that experts omit nearly 70% of procedural and decision-related information when relying on ordinary recall. Even thinking aloud may be incomplete because some of the knowledge guiding action is no longer readily accessible to consciousness.

This creates an “expert blind spot.” The person most qualified to perform the work may struggle to describe everything that makes their performance so successful.

It also creates a trap for AI teams. A polished interview transcript can look comprehensive, the expert’s account is coherent, the analyst has captured clear themes and the model can summarise them fluently. None of this tells us what was left unsaid.

CTA uses more deliberate questions to uncover what an ordinary interview misses.

One is the Critical Decision Method, which begins with a specific, difficult incident rather than a request for a general process. The expert reconstructs the event through several passes. The interviewer establishes a timeline, returns to decision points and probes for cues, expectations, alternatives and uncertainty.

Instead of asking, “How do you normally assess a claim?”, we might ask:

  • Tell me about a case that initially appeared routine but was not.
  • When did your understanding of the situation change?
  • What did you notice at that moment?
  • What were you expecting to see?
  • Which option looked plausible but would have been wrong?
  • What would a less-experienced assessor have missed?
  • If one piece of evidence had been absent, would your decision have changed?

These questions are useful because expertise often becomes visible through contrast. The routine case explains the rule; the difficult case reveals the model behind it.

Good interviewers also learn to notice apparently simple cognitive verbs: recognise, assess, notice, realise, suspect. Each can conceal an entire operation.

“I determined that the evidence was insufficient” is not the end of an explanation. It is the beginning of one.

Knowledge representation is an architectural decision

CTA does not end when the interview is complete. Elicited knowledge has to be represented for some purpose.

In an agentic system, this is not a single technical decision. Different kinds of expert knowledge belong in different parts of the architecture.

A stable calculation may become deterministic code. A policy definition may belong in retrieval. A categorical distinction might become an ontology. A repeated judgment could be expressed through examples or model instructions. A hard constraint may need to be enforced through tool permissions rather than entrusted to the model.

Other findings might become:

  • preconditions for calling a tool;
  • tests in an evaluation dataset;
  • expected evidence for a decision;
  • a requirement to request more information;
  • a human approval point;
  • an escalation rule;
  • a trace field that makes the agent’s basis for action inspectable.

Recent work on codifying engineering expertise illustrates why this separation matters. Researchers found that explicit procedural rules could be implemented reliably in code, while contextual design principles needed more flexible representation through prompts and retrieval. Neither approach was sufficient alone. Code lacked contextual judgment; the language model produced inappropriate results without explicit constraints.

In other words, where we place the uncovered knowledge reflects what we believe about it.

When we put a rule in a prompt, we are saying the model may interpret it. When we enforce it in code, we are saying it must hold. When we create an approval gate, we are saying the system is not authorised to resolve that judgment independently.

These choices say something about what the system may interpret, what it must obey and what it is not allowed to decide.

A good agent inherits expert restraint

Most attempts to transfer expertise focus on what the expert concludes, but equally important is when the expert does not conclude.

Experienced practitioners know when a familiar pattern is weak, when important evidence is absent and when the consequences justify another opinion. Their expertise includes stopping, checking, escalating and remaining uncertain.

An agent that reproduces expert answers without expert restraint has learned the most dangerous half of the job.

So the interview also needs to ask about uncertainty:

  • What makes you less confident in this decision?
  • When would you seek another person’s judgment?
  • Which missing evidence would prevent you from acting?
  • What is the cost of waiting compared with the cost of being wrong?
  • Have you encountered a case where the usual rule failed?
  • What would make you reverse this decision?

Those answers need to shape both the agent and the tests used to evaluate it.

We should not only test whether the agent reaches the expected outcome. We should test whether it requests evidence when evidence is missing, distinguishes ambiguity from contradiction, and escalates cases whose consequences exceed its authority.

The prototype is also an elicitation tool

It is tempting to imagine discovery as a phase that ends before implementation begins: interview the experts, document the requirements and build the agent. In practice, though, the prototype often reveals more about expertise than the initial interviews.

An expert can react to a concrete agent decision in ways they cannot react to an abstract process map. They can point to the cue the system ignored, the exception it applied too broadly or the moment at which it should have stopped. Disagreement creates a new elicitation opportunity.

The prototype is not just testing the software. It is also testing the team’s understanding of the work. A failure may reveal missing knowledge, incorrect data, a rule placed in the wrong part of the system or an assumption that no longer holds.

The loop should look something like this:

  • observe and interview;
  • construct a representation;
  • implement it;
  • replay real cases;
  • inspect disagreements and failures;
  • return to experts with concrete traces;
  • revise the representation.

Why this links to the sudden explosion in forward-deployed engineers

The rise of forward-deployed engineering is another sign that knowledge transfer — not raw model capability — is becoming the constraint.

OpenAI, Anthropic and AWS are among the companies building teams that work directly with customers on production deployments. These roles combine technical discovery, system design, implementation and evaluation.

Much of their value comes from being close to the work.

Forward-deployed engineers see the difference between the process described in a meeting and the process that survives contact with real work. They encounter undocumented systems, local terminology, informal coordination and exceptions that appear only under operational pressure. They can feed those discoveries into both the deployed system and the underlying product.

Palantir describes this process as the ‘human equivalent of backpropagation’: information from the field flows back into the product.

The metaphor is useful, but the engineer is the one doing much of the learning. By working alongside the domain team, they notice what was missing from the requirements and translate it into something the system can use.

Agent teams may benefit from seeing this as a cognitive-science capability rather than simply a new engineering job title. Knowing how to build an agent is not enough. Someone needs to know how to study the work.

Deployment creates a new cognitive task

There is one final complication — once an agent enters a workflow, the workflow itself changes.

People may stop performing some checks because they believe the agent has completed them. They may develop new skills in reviewing its output or new shortcuts for working around it. Exceptions may shift. Responsibility may become ambiguous. The records generated after deployment may reflect the agent’s previous decisions, creating a feedback loop between the system and the evidence used to evaluate it.

The original CTA described a human task. The deployed system is now a joint cognitive system.

This means the team may need to perform CTA again — not only to determine whether the agent copied the old workflow correctly, but to understand how cognition, attention and responsibility are now distributed between people and the agent.

Where does situational awareness live? Who notices deterioration? What does the human still know how to do? When the agent is wrong, is the person reviewing it equipped to recognise the error?

These questions cannot be answered by evaluating the model in isolation.

The agent is a working hypothesis

We should be careful with the language of “capturing” expertise. It suggests that knowledge can be extracted intact from a person, placed inside a system and scaled without loss.

What we actually build is a partial, purpose-specific representation of judgment.

It may perform well across known cases. It may allow less-experienced people to achieve better outcomes. It may reduce demands on scarce experts. These are meaningful achievements, but they are not evidence that the system now possesses the expert’s experience.

A more useful question is not

Have we captured the expert’s knowledge?

It is:

Which judgments can this system reproduce, under which conditions, using which evidence — and does it know when those conditions no longer hold?

Cognitive task analysis cannot answer every engineering question involved in building an agent. But it can help us ask better questions before we encode an incomplete account of work into a system that acts.

An agent will never contain everything an expert knows. What it can contain is a visible, testable account of the judgments it has been designed to reproduce — one that can be challenged and revised as the work changes.
