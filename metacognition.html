Research note — 001

What does Apple’s latest paper tell us about metacognition in AI?

15 June 2025
8 min read

Artificial intelligence
Cognition
Metacognition

“Thinking about our thinking” shapes human cognition—and it seems to be missing in current large language models.

At first glance, Apple’s research paper The Illusion of Thinking might seem like another technical evaluation of large reasoning models: systems that appear to work through problems step by step before producing an answer.

But buried in its controversial findings is something more than a performance comparison. The research highlights a fundamental difference between human and artificial intelligence that is often missing from mainstream discussion: current AI models lack genuine metacognitive capabilities. They cannot think about their thinking.

What is metacognition—and why does it matter?

Metacognition is our awareness and control of our own cognitive processes. It has two key components. The first is monitoring: our ability to assess our own knowledge and mental state. The second is control: using those assessments to guide what we do next.

When you realise you do not understand something, you might slow down, reread it, try another approach, or seek more information. Decades of psychological research show that people can assess their confidence, recognise gaps in knowledge, and allocate effort in response.

Large language models can display behaviours that resemble reflection, retrieval, and step-by-step reasoning—but they usually need to be prompted to do so. They do not independently control these behaviours in the way a metacognitively aware system would.

The model stopped thinking—but why?

Apple’s researchers tested reasoning models on puzzles of varying complexity. One striking result was that models reduced their reasoning tokens after complexity passed a certain threshold. As the problem became harder, the model appeared to stop thinking.

Humans often respond to difficulty by allocating more cognitive resources: slowing down, planning, breaking a problem apart, or changing strategy. We can also give up, of course. But even giving up may follow an assessment of difficulty, ability, and likely reward.

The observed model behaviour appears reactive. It offers no clear evidence that the system recognised the problem’s difficulty and deliberately selected a strategy appropriate to its constraints.

Overthinking simple problems

The second interesting finding was “overthinking” on simpler problems. Models sometimes found a correct solution early, then continued exploring alternatives until they moved away from the right answer.

Humans use metacognitive signals to judge when they have probably found an answer and when further search is worthwhile. A model has no equivalent feeling of knowing. It continues generating what is statistically likely next without independently assessing whether that generation serves a useful purpose.

Can current architectures bridge the gap?

Researchers have explored computational metacognition for decades, but many approaches fall short of the real-time self-monitoring and adaptive control that characterise human metacognition. Treating metacognition as more inference by the same system may not be enough.

A genuinely intelligent system would need to recognise increasing difficulty, understand the limits of its knowledge, choose an appropriate reasoning strategy, and know when it has succeeded. These are not minor refinements. They shape whether a system can learn safely and act reliably.

Knowing what you know

A system that solves complex problems but cannot recognise when it is struggling, when it has succeeded, or when it should change approaches remains fundamentally limited.

Perhaps the path to more capable AI lies not only in building systems that can think, but in building systems that know that they think—and can recognise when their thinking falls short.
