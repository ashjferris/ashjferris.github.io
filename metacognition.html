What does Apple’s latest paper tell us about metacognition in AI?

15 June 2025
8 min read

Artificial intelligence
Cognition
Metacognition

‘Thinking about our thinking’ shapes human cognition — and it seems to be missing in current Large Language Models (LLMs).

At first glance, Apple’s recent research paper “The Illusion of Thinking” might seem like yet another technical evaluation of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) — the latest generation of Large Language Models (LLMs) that appear to ‘think’ through problems step by step, generating detailed reasoning traces before providing their answer.

But buried in Apple’s somewhat controversial findings is something more than simply a performance comparison. For me, at least, their research highlighted a fundamental difference between human and AI that seems to be missing from mainstream discussions about artificial intelligence. Current AI models lack genuine metacognitive capabilities — they can’t think about their thinking.

Depending on your level of knowledge of these models, this might already be obvious. Given that LLMs are simply probabilistic models, generating the next most likely token based on the context, for some it might seem absurd to conflate their performance with human intelligence. Others may believe we are already well on our way to Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), also sometimes called ‘human level intelligence’.

Regardless of which side you sit on, at a minimum these models give the ‘illusion of intelligence’. Developments over the last few years have meant LLMs sound pretty damn smart, and outperform humans on a multitude of benchmarks. But what do they lack that prevents them from demonstrating true intelligence? Many things, maybe, but metacognition is definitely one of them.

What Is Metacognition and Why Does It Matter?

To understand what’s missing, we first need to talk about something humans possess that AI doesn’t: metacognition. Simply put, metacognition is “thinking about thinking” — our awareness and control of our own cognitive processes.

Metacognition has two key components. First is monitoring — our ability to assess our own knowledge and cognitive states. This is what allows you to experience that “tip-of-the-tongue” feeling when you know you know something but can’t quite retrieve it. Second is control — using those assessments to guide our behaviour. When you realise you don’t understand a concept, you might slow down, re-read the material, or seek additional information.

The evidence for human metacognitive abilities spans decades of research. Classic studies demonstrate that people can accurately judge their own performance and assess their confidence in decisions. Brain imaging reveals that metacognitive monitoring involves specific neural networks, particularly in the prefrontal cortex.

Perhaps most tellingly, when these metacognitive systems are damaged — such as in certain types of brain injury — people lose the ability to monitor their own cognitive performance. They might feel confident about incorrect answers or fail to recognise when they need help.

Even non-human primates show metacognitive abilities. Rhesus monkeys can make both retrospective and prospective metacognitive judgements, choosing low-risk options when uncertain and high-risk options when confident.

Metacognition also interacts with many other cognitive processes we have going on in our complicated brains. If we encounter a new problem we have not seen before, we might purposefully think about other similar problems we have previously encountered (also known as retrieval) to help us solve this new problem. Interestingly, if we prompt LLMs to do this too, it increases their performance.

The problem is, LLMs are capable of showing many characteristics of human reasoning — working through a problem step by step, self-reflection on answers, using retrieval to assist with problem solving, and more. This is not entirely surprising, since these models are trained on vast amounts of human text and language — so much that it would take a human at least 170,000 years to read it all. However, the models need to be prompted to do so — they cannot control this behaviour. For all their complexities, they lack the ability to think about their thinking on their own.

This metacognitive ability shapes how humans approach problems of varying difficulty — and it seems to be missing in current models.

Back to what the Apple paper tell us

Apple’s researchers tested several models on controllable puzzle environments such as the Tower of Hanoi, systematically varying problem complexity while analysing both final answers and internal reasoning traces (essentially the models’ train of thought). There were two findings that were particularly interesting in the context of AI ‘thinking’ and metacognition.

The model stopped thinking — but why?

Firstly, the paper found that the models actually reduced their thinking tokens as the problem complexity increased past a particular threshold — when the problem got too hard, they stopped thinking.

Now, when humans encounter a difficult problem, we naturally recognise the increased challenge and usually respond by allocating more cognitive resources — we slow down, think more carefully, perhaps try different approaches. If we are being pessimistic, we could also say that we might simply give up if a problem is too hard — which feels similar to what the models did in Apple’s paper.

It’s worth noting that some have rebutted Apple’s claim that the models reached a point of ‘complete accuracy collapse’, stating that on the Tower of Hanoi puzzle in particular, the tokens the model would need to solve the puzzle is larger than the maximum number of tokens the model can output. Without seeing the reasoning traces, it’s hard to say anything for certain — maybe the models recognised that they did not have adequate tokens to solve the puzzle, and ended their thinking there.

However, even if this were true for the Tower of Hanoi puzzle as well as the other three puzzles tested (which did not have the same limitations and also saw this result), I still view this result as evidence of metacognitive blindness in the model. The observed behaviour appears purely reactive. Regardless of the why, the models reduced thinking after encountering the difficult puzzle, rather than proactively recognising the problem is difficult, and selecting appropriate reasoning strategies given the constraints.

When faced with the same problem, humans might engage in strategic planning — considering constraints, breaking it into subproblems, asking for more information, and choosing reasoning approaches accordingly. Unless reasoning traces are released and they reveal otherwise, to me the models show no evidence of this kind of monitoring of problem difficulty and control over reasoning strategy selection that we would expect to see in metacognition.

Overthinking Simple Problems

The second interesting finding from the paper was “overthinking” on simpler problems. The models would often find correct solutions early but then continue exploring incorrect alternatives, essentially talking themselves out of the right answer. Calling this ‘overthinking’, however, may be treating these models as more human-like than they are.

Humans possess what psychologists call the “feeling of knowing” — we can sense when we know something but have not been able to retrieve it yet. It’s the feeling of “I know I know this, I just have to think harder”. We also use metacognition to tell us when we’ve found the right answer and when to stop searching, as we feel confidence in our correctness. We have mechanisms to recognise when we’re going in circles or pursuing unproductive reasoning.

This finding highlights the fact that these models lack such ability. They cannot recognise when they’ve successfully solved a problem, nor can they detect when their reasoning has gone astray. They continue processing without any assessment of whether that generation serves a useful purpose, as long as it is statistically the next most likely token.

Can current architectures bridge this gap?

The field has seen multiple attempts to build metacognitive capabilities into AI. A recent systematic review of computational metacognitive architectures reveals that while researchers have explored various approaches to building metacognitive AI over the past three decades, most efforts fall short of the real-time self-monitoring and adaptive control that characterises human metacognition. Current approaches often treat LLM metacognition as “inference about inference” — essentially using the same transformer architecture to reason about reasoning, rather than having specialised metacognitive circuits.

Leading AI researchers argue that current transformer-based architectures, like what powers the popular chat models of today, are fundamentally insufficient for achieving human-level intelligence. Yann LeCun, Meta’s Chief AI scientist, has been particularly vocal about these limitations. LeCun argues that “LLMs really are not capable” of key intelligent behaviours including “understanding of the physical world; persistent memory; reasoning; and complex planning capabilities”.

LeCun’s critique is particularly relevant to metacognition. He notes that humans have “a special piece of our brain that we use as working memory. LLMs don’t have that”. This working memory is crucial for metacognitive monitoring — it’s what allows us to hold our current reasoning state in mind while simultaneously evaluating its quality.

Apple’s findings regarding reduced reasoning effort support LeCun’s broader argument. A truly intelligent system would recognise increasing difficulty and control the strategies it uses — it would also know when it has reached the correct answer. Further to this, a metacognitively aware system would understand the limits of its knowledge, and would be able to engage in self-regulated learning. To learn something new, you need to be able to recognise that there is something new to be learnt.

The implications of a metacognitively blind ‘intelligence’

The metacognitive blindness highlighted by Apple’s paper has real-world implications. When AI models cannot recognise their own limitations, they pose significant risks in high-stakes situations.

A recent study published in Nature Communications examined metacognitive abilities in medical reasoning tasks, providing further evidence that current LLMs exhibit “significant metacognitive deficiencies across all tested models”. The researchers tested twelve different models on medical questions and found that “models consistently failed to recognise their knowledge limitations and provided confident answers even when correct options were absent.”

This phenomenon parallels the Dunning-Kruger effect in human psychology, where people with limited knowledge overestimate their competence. The original research found that “the knowledge and skills necessary for good performance are often the same knowledge and skills necessary to recognise good performance.”

Humans at least have the potential for metacognitive development — we can learn to recognise our strengths and limitations. It seems that LLMs, given their current architecture, lack even this potential.

Knowing what you know

Addressing these metacognitive limitations will require changes to how we approach AI development. While much attention focuses on scaling models larger and training them on more data, the ability to “know what you know” may be far more critical for genuine intelligence.

A system that can solve complex problems but cannot recognise when it’s struggling, when it has succeeded, or when it needs to change approaches is fundamentally limited in ways that no amount of additional training data or computational power can address.

Perhaps the path to truly intelligent AI lies not in building systems that can think, but in building systems that know that they think — and more importantly, that can recognise when their thinking falls short.
